# Guidance by manuscript component

## Title and keywords

Represent the actual object, method, and scope. Remove empty `Research on`/`Study of` framing where it adds nothing, but do not delete scope-bearing words such as `analysis`, `survey`, or `experimental`. Prefer informative brevity over slogans. Do not add `novel`, `first`, or `optimal` without support; flag unsupported claims already present. Retain qualifications such as partial observation or a class of systems. Title case and length follow the venue. Keywords should be searchable established concepts; do not mechanically singularize terms that conventionally use plural forms.

## Abstract

Check whether the supplied text communicates the problem, approach, main result, and significance within its scope. An abstract should normally make sense on its own. Avoid unnecessary cross-references and unexplained abbreviations; venue requirements govern permitted citations or formulas. Removing a citation must not erase essential attribution. Retain quantitative results already supplied, including baseline and conditions. If results or comparisons are missing, ask for them; do not fabricate a result sentence. Do not pad with generic field importance or formulaic novelty.

## Introduction and related work

Look for a supported path from problem and relevant background to a specific gap, approach, and contribution. Distinguish the current work from cited work without disparagement or exaggerated novelty. Cluster related findings only if supplied content supports the grouping. Avoid a repetitive author-by-author inventory when deep editing is authorized.

Keep citations attached to the correct claims. `The method in [12]` or a named author can provide a grammatical subject; do not mechanically replace every citation with the same formula. Do not infer the absence of previous research from silence in the excerpt. Formatting references is distinct from verifying their existence or contents.

## Methods and mathematical development

Identify the problem, assumptions, inputs, operations, and outputs from the supplied material. Explain why a step follows when the draft already provides the reason. Separate formal definitions, results, proof steps, and explanatory intuition. Use the technical-integrity reference for notation and logic. Do not remove apparently repetitive implementation details that support reproducibility without considering their function.

## Results and discussion

Keep observation separate from explanation and conjecture. Preserve the evaluation conditions, comparator, metric, units, uncertainty, and limitations. Do not convert a numerical improvement into statistical significance, a simulation into an experiment, or measured runtime into an asymptotic complexity claim. If `better`, `efficient`, or `robust` lacks a criterion, query it rather than choose one.

## Conclusion

Summarize what the work establishes and under what limitations. Avoid merely repeating the abstract. Retain supplied implications and future directions without presenting future work as completed. Do not invent limitations to satisfy a checklist. Self-contained wording is desirable, but exact cross-reference restrictions depend on the venue.

## Captions and references

Make captions identify the depicted quantity or comparison with enough supplied context to interpret it. Keep panel labels, units, figure numbers, and statistical qualifiers intact. Do not change a citation system, numbering, title case, punctuation, or bibliography order based on lecture examples alone. Request or verify the target style when exact conformance is requested. Do not invent missing DOIs, pages, authors, or dates.

## Existing reviewer responses

Preserve the reviewer's comment verbatim unless the user asks otherwise. Distinguish the answer, evidence or rationale, and actual manuscript action. Keep the reply respectful and direct; do not turn a justified disagreement into a concession. State `we have added` only when the supplied context establishes that the addition occurred. Never invent line/page numbers, new experiments, or acceptance of all reviewer requests. A language pass is not a rebuttal audit or simulated review.
