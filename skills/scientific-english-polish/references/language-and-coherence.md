# Language and coherence

Apply these checks to the passage; they are not mandatory visible sections in the response.

## Grammar with technical context

- Find the grammatical subject before correcting verb number, especially through long modifiers. Treat a symbol according to its stated referent, not its typography.
- Check countability and singular/plural forms. Choose `a` or `an` by pronunciation, including acronyms. Choose `the` by identifiability in context, not simply whether the noun appeared earlier. An object can be identifiable on first mention.
- Check antecedents of `it`, `they`, `this`, `which`, and `the former/latter`. Replace a pronoun with a specific term when several antecedents are plausible; do not invent that term's meaning.
- Repair sentence fragments and comma splices. A dependent clause starting with because, although, if, or since needs an appropriate main clause. Mathematical displays can form part of a grammatical sentence.
- For a participial opener, identify who performs the action. Prefer an explicit actor or conditional clause when attachment is misleading. Adding `by` alone is not a general repair for a dangling modifier.
- Keep coordinated items comparable in grammar and conceptual level. Preserve asymmetric scientific relationships rather than forcing artificial parallelism.
- Check conventional collocations and literal Chinese translations. Translate the intended concept: 迭代次数 concerns iterations, not algebra; increase by and increase to have different numerical meanings.
- Relative-clause punctuation can change scope. Decide whether the clause identifies a subset or adds supplementary information before choosing punctuation or that/which.

## Clarity without changing claims

Place the core subject and action where readers can find them. Split an overloaded sentence at genuine logical boundaries, retaining conditions and citations beside the claims they qualify. Prefer concrete, familiar verbs over ornamental vocabulary; do not replace a precise established term with a fashionable synonym.

Remove empty framing when it carries no needed context. `The problem of X is studied` can often become `We study X`, but preserve the author's chosen voice if it works. Avoid inventing sweeping background claims to replace clumsy background claims.

Each paragraph should develop a recognizable point. Connect given information to new information, then evidence to interpretation. Paragraph size depends on content and layout, not a fixed number of printed lines. Preserve paragraph structure in light mode.

Do not add `therefore`, `consequently`, or `because` merely to make prose flow. If the relationship is unknown, use a neutral transition or ask the author. Distinguish contrast (`however`), concession (`although`), sequence (`then`), and purpose (`to`).

## Tense, voice, and terminology

Choose tense by communicative function and venue: established properties and a paper's contents often use present tense; completed procedures and observed results often use past tense; a developing body of work can use present perfect. Coherence does not require one tense throughout a manuscript.

Use active voice when it makes agency clear and passive voice when the operation or object is the topic. Switching voices is permissible when it serves information flow. Do not conflate third person with passive voice: `This paper presents` is active.

Expand unfamiliar abbreviations on first use in the relevant independent reading unit, often abstract and main text separately. Preserve conventional technical acronyms and author-approved names. Avoid new abbreviations used only once or twice, but impose no fixed quota and never shorten an established algorithm name by inventing a new acronym.

For formal scientific prose, prefer expanded contractions where appropriate. Follow an existing consistent spelling convention. Preserve exact reference titles and names even if their spelling differs from manuscript style.
